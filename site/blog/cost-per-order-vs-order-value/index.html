What should an order cost? Why EGP 661 can be cheap and EGP 400 expensive

"How much should an order cost?" has no single answer. In our accounts, the most expensive orders came with the highest returns. Here's how cost per order and order value work together.

Islam Mohamed Abdelaziz Founder & CEO, CTRL Solutions 21 November 20263 min read

Brand owners often ask me what a good cost per order is. They expect a number. My answer is a question: what is the order worth?

Six campaigns, six different answers

Here are some of the best campaigns from accounts we run, with what each order cost and what each order was worth on average:

  • A perfume store: about EGP 201 per order, average order about EGP 1,272, return 6.33X.
  • A perfume brand: about EGP 272 per order, average order about EGP 1,244, return 4.57X.
  • A newer perfume store: about EGP 276 per order, average order about EGP 1,121, return 4.07X.
  • The same perfume brand's biggest campaign: about EGP 361 per order, average order about EGP 1,614, return 4.47X.
  • A laptop store: about EGP 661 per order, average order about EGP 14,547, return 22X.
  • A copy of that laptop campaign: about EGP 888 per order, average order about EGP 11,644, return 13.1X.

Source: Meta Ads Manager, lifetime campaign data pulled on 14 November 2026. Sales are attributed by Meta. Brand names hidden.

The most expensive orders on this list came with the highest returns. A laptop order at EGP 661 was a bargain. The same EGP 661 for a perfume order worth about EGP 1,250 would be a return of under 2X, and for most perfume businesses, a loss.

The formula behind it

Return on ad spend is simply the average order value divided by the cost per order. EGP 1,244 per order at EGP 272 each returns about 4.6X. EGP 14,547 per order at EGP 661 each returns 22X. That's why comparing cost per order between different products, or between different brands, tells you almost nothing.

How to find the cost per order you can afford

Start from what's left after an order is delivered:

  1. Take the average order value.
  2. Subtract the product cost, packaging and delivery.
  3. Subtract the cost of refused and returned orders, spread across the delivered ones.
  4. What remains is your break-even cost per order. Your target should sit comfortably below it.

Here's an illustration with round numbers, not a real client's margins. If a perfume order averages EGP 1,250 and EGP 500 is left after costs, EGP 500 is the break-even. At EGP 272 per order, every order earns about EGP 228 after ads. At EGP 661, every order loses about EGP 161.

Be careful with account-level numbers

Account totals mix campaigns with different goals. The laptop store's account, for example, shows about EGP 1,111 of spend per order over its lifetime, because that spend includes messaging campaigns whose results are conversations, not purchases. Judge sales campaigns by their own numbers and messaging campaigns by theirs. I describe that account in 48,913 conversations for a laptop store.

What this means for your store

  • Low-ticket products need efficient ads or bigger baskets. If your average order is small, raising it is often easier than cutting ad costs. I covered that in raising average order value.
  • High-ticket products can afford expensive results. Laptops and other expensive products can pay more per order, per click and per conversation, and still win.
  • Set targets per product, not per account. A store selling a EGP 400 item and a EGP 4,000 item needs two different targets.
  • Count delivered orders. In cash-on-delivery stores, refused orders raise the real cost of every delivered one.
