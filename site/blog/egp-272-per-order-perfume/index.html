EGP 272 per order: a perfume campaign with 86 orders at 4.57X

Among the larger campaigns in our biggest perfume account, one brought orders at the lowest cost: EGP 272 each. It's a great number, and a good lesson in reading cost per order together with order value.

Islam Mohamed Abdelaziz Founder & CEO, CTRL Solutions 21 November 20263 min read

In the perfume brand account behind most of our sales numbers, one sales campaign launched in late February 2026 stands out for one reason: its cost per order.

Source: Meta Ads Manager, lifetime campaign data pulled on 14 November 2026. Sales are attributed by Meta. The brand's name is hidden.

The campaign spent EGP 23,412.70, and Meta attributed EGP 106,954 in sales to it from 86 orders. That's EGP 272 per order and a 4.57X return.

How it compares with the rest of the account

Across the account's whole lifetime, an order cost about EGP 400 and the average order was about EGP 1,450. I described the account in EGP 4.65M for one perfume brand. In its seven biggest campaigns, an order cost between roughly EGP 308 and EGP 438.

So EGP 272 is the lowest cost per order among the account's larger campaigns. On that number alone, it looks like the best campaign in the account.

What the cost per order hides

Here's the part I find more useful. Divide this campaign's sales by its orders and the average order was about EGP 1,244, lower than the account's EGP 1,450.

Compare it with the account's biggest campaign, launched in March. It paid about EGP 361 per order, roughly a third more, but its average order was about EGP 1,614. Both campaigns landed close to 4.5X: 4.57X for the cheaper orders and 4.47X for the bigger ones.

Cheaper orders and smaller baskets often come together. A campaign can find buyers who order one bottle at a lower cost, or buyers who order more at a higher cost. Which one is better depends on the margin, not on which number looks nicer in Ads Manager.

Why smaller budgets often get cheaper orders

At around EGP 23,000, Meta can spend most of the budget on the people most likely to buy. As the budget grows, it has to reach further, and the cost of each order usually rises. That's why I don't expect a campaign at EGP 80,000 to keep the cost per order it had at EGP 20,000. I explain how I scale in scaling a winner without killing it.

How I'd use a number like EGP 272

  • Start from your break-even cost per order. Take the average order, then subtract product cost, delivery and the cost of refused orders. What's left is the most you can pay for an order without losing money.
  • Read cost per order together with order value. Here, ad spend was about 22% of attributed sales. That ratio, not EGP 272 alone, tells you whether the campaign is healthy.
  • Count delivered orders, not only placed orders. In cash-on-delivery stores, some orders are refused at the door. The real cost per order is spend divided by orders that were delivered and paid.
  • Don't chase the cheapest order. If cheap orders come with smaller baskets, an offer that raises the average order can be worth more than a lower cost per order. I covered that in raising average order value.

The lesson

A low cost per order is a good sign, not a verdict. This campaign was excellent: EGP 272 per order, 86 orders and a 4.57X return. But the account's bigger campaigns, with more expensive orders and bigger baskets, delivered almost the same return at more than three times the spend. Both are wins. They just win in different ways.
