Meta ads for cash-on-delivery stores in Egypt: the playbook I actually use

Cash on delivery changes the math of every campaign. This is the order I set things up in for a new store, from the unit economics to the phone call after the order.

Islam Mohamed Abdelaziz Founder & CEO, CTRL Solutions 21 November 20263 min read

Most Meta ads advice is written for markets where people pay by card. In Egypt, most online orders are paid in cash at the door, and that changes everything: an "order" is a promise, not a sale. Some customers won't answer the phone, some will change their minds, and some will refuse the parcel.

This is the playbook I use for cash-on-delivery stores, in the order I set it up.

1. Start with what an order can cost

Before launching anything, I calculate the break-even cost per order. The simple version: take the order value, subtract product cost and delivery cost, then multiply by the share of orders that actually get delivered. That's the most you can pay for an order and still not lose money.

Example numbers, just to show the math: a EGP 1,000 order with EGP 400 of product cost and EGP 70 of delivery leaves EGP 530. If 80% of orders get delivered, the break-even cost per order is about EGP 424, before returns and overheads. Your target should sit well below that.

Every account gets its own target. A laptop store can pay far more per order than a perfume store, which I explain in what an order should cost.

2. Fix tracking before spending

In COD, the purchase happens when the order is submitted, so that's when the Purchase event should fire, with the right value and currency. I set up the Meta pixel together with the Conversions API so events still arrive when the browser blocks the pixel, and I make sure the two don't double count. Without clean purchase data, Meta optimizes toward people who click, not people who buy. Details in Pixel and Conversions API for COD.

3. Keep the structure simple

For most stores I start with very little:

  • One sales campaign for new customers, with broad targeting and several creatives.
  • One retargeting campaign for people who viewed products or started an order.
  • A messaging campaign only if the brand actually sells through WhatsApp or Messenger and has people ready to answer.

Splitting a small budget across many campaigns starves all of them. Consolidated budgets learn faster.

4. Creatives do the targeting now

With broad targeting, the creative decides who stops scrolling. I test new creatives every week, and each one has to answer the COD buyer's questions in seconds: what is it, why is it worth the price, is it original, and how fast does it arrive. Short videos with a strong first three seconds usually lead. More in how I test creatives every week.

5. Make ordering effortless

The landing page must load fast on a mobile connection and show the same offer the ad promised. The order form should ask only for name, phone, city and address. Every extra field loses orders.

6. Win the order after the order

This is the step most advertisers ignore. Confirm every order quickly, by a call or a WhatsApp message, while the buyer still remembers why they ordered. Remind them of the delivery date. A fast confirmation protects your delivery rate, and your delivery rate protects your real return.

7. Judge by delivered revenue

Ads Manager reports submitted orders. Your bank account sees delivered ones. Every week I compare the two and adjust the targets. A campaign at 4X submitted can be a loser if half its orders never arrive.

What good looks like

For reference, on a perfume brand we run, seven campaigns that each spent between EGP 37,000 and EGP 81,000 all landed between 4.17X and 4.64X. On another perfume store, one campaign brought 168 orders from EGP 33,700 of spend at 6.3X. Those results came from this exact boring order of operations, repeated.
