When to kill an ad: the 48-hour rhythm I use

Most bad ads don't fail loudly. They just keep spending. Here's the rhythm and the rules I use to decide which ads stay, which get another day, and which go.

Islam Mohamed Abdelaziz Founder & CEO, CTRL Solutions 21 November 20263 min read

There are two ways to waste money on Meta ads. The obvious one is leaving a bad ad running for a week. The less obvious one is killing ads after a few hours, before they've had a chance, and never finding the winner that was in the batch.

The rhythm I use sits in between: every live ad gets reviewed every 48 hours, and every decision follows rules agreed before the money is spent.

Why 48 hours

  • The first hours are noisy. Delivery starts unevenly, and a handful of clicks can make any ad look brilliant or terrible.
  • Buyers take time. Many people see an ad, think about it and order hours later, so early numbers tend to undercount.
  • Two full days include mornings, evenings and late nights, which often behave very differently.
  • Changing things too often hurts. Significant edits can send a campaign back into learning. A fixed review rhythm stops the urge to touch everything every hour.

But 48 hours is a rhythm, not a magic number. The real trigger is how much an ad has spent compared with your target cost per order.

The rules

1. No purchase after one and a half to two times the target cost per order. If other ads in the same ad set are converting and this one isn't, it gets paused. If your target is EGP 400 per order, that's somewhere around EGP 600 to EGP 800 of spend.

2. Purchases above target, with strong early signals. If people stop on the ad, click and add to cart, but the cost per order is high, the ad may need a better offer or a better landing page, not a funeral. It gets another review cycle, or a new version with a different opening.

3. Cost per order inside the target. It stays. If it holds for several days, it becomes a candidate for scaling, which I explain in scaling a winner without killing it.

4. A winner that's fading. Frequency rising, click-through rate falling, cost per order creeping up for three days or more. That's creative fatigue. I replace or refresh the creative instead of killing the whole campaign.

The numbers I check, in order

  1. Spend against the target cost per order: has the ad spent enough to judge?
  2. Hook rate: do people stop in the first seconds?
  3. Link click-through rate and cost per click: do they want to know more?
  4. Cost per add to cart or form start: are they serious?
  5. Cost per purchase and return on ad spend: the numbers that pay.

When an ad fails, this order tells me where it failed. A weak hook needs a new opening. Strong clicks with no orders point to the offer, the price or the page. I explain how we test in weekly creative testing.

Messaging campaigns need their own thresholds

For campaigns that start conversations instead of orders, I judge the cost per conversation and the quality of the chats. And I don't use one threshold for every campaign. In a laptop store we run, conversations from one retargeting campaign cost about EGP 4.37 each, while the store's biggest messaging campaign paid about EGP 14.72. Both can be healthy: people who already know a brand are usually cheaper to reach, and a big campaign has to reach further. Judging both by one threshold would either kill good campaigns or overpay for easy ones. I wrote about that account in 48,913 conversations for a laptop store.

Ads Manager isn't the final judge in COD

In cash-on-delivery stores, a purchase in Ads Manager is an order placed, not an order paid. Before I call an ad a winner, I want to know whether its orders are being confirmed and delivered. An ad that brings cheap orders that get refused at the door isn't cheap. I'll go deeper on this in CPA, ROAS and delivered orders.

The point of rules

Rules aren't about being strict. They protect the budget from two feelings: hope, which keeps bad ads running, and panic, which kills good ones too early.
